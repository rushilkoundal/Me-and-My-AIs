## Content purpose

**I turn my personal experiences with AI—its mistakes, weird responses, overconfidence and frustrating behaviour—into animated parody comedy, bringing the AI models to life as characters.**

Each episode starts from a specific AI behaviour or experience the creator recognises firsthand. Give that behaviour a physical or conversational expression through the characters. A shared-home or roommate relationship is not the series premise. Choose a setting that makes the incident and its consequence clear.

## Audience priority

**Make people recognise an experience strongly enough that they want to send the Short to someone.** The intended response is “This happened to me”, “This is exactly you” or “This explains what I have been complaining about”. Sharing with a friend, family member or colleague should grow from the emotion and joke.

- Start with a specific frustration or expectation, such as asking for one tiny edit and losing everything that was already right.
- Show the emotional progression: hope or trust, the AI response, recognition of the mistake, then a satisfying comic consequence or reaction.
- Identify who would naturally receive the clip and why. It must make sense without knowing previous episodes or reading the description.
- Do not ask viewers to like, comment, subscribe, share or tag someone in dialogue, on-screen text or an end card. Avoid indirect engagement bait too.
- Likes and comments can suggest recognition, but they do not establish that people shared the clip.

Before scripting, record the real incident, the AI behaviour being parodied, the emotion the viewer should recognise, and the likely recipient/reason for sharing. If these are vague, sharpen the premise before production. Random AI chaos without a recognisable source behaviour is not enough.

## Initial comparison

Test six new comparable incidents, three per format, alternating the formats. Use a **12–15 second working target**, similar character scale and cover treatment, and a consistent publishing slot. Keep the AI behaviour and premise clarity comparable. Duration is an editorial target, not a platform threshold; do not damage a joke to hit an exact second count.

Compare each Short at the same first-24-hour and seven-day age. The seven-day comparison is the main decision point:

- Primary outcome: actual platform-reported shares, shown as raw counts and **share events per 1,000 engaged views** on YouTube. This is an event rate, not a percentage of unique people who shared.
- Supporting measures: engaged views, feed exposure when available, stayed-to-watch, average view duration/percentage, and repeat viewing where measured. These establish reach and comprehension alongside sharing.
- Likes, comments and subscriber changes remain secondary evidence. Use comments to understand which emotions and experiences viewers recognise.
- Keep YouTube and Instagram results separate. Use reported shares/sends and an explicitly stated denominator for each platform. Missing data remains unknown.
- Compare several episodes and the median share rate, keeping raw counts and audience size visible. One share or a barely exposed clip does not decide the winner. If evidence is sparse or split, continue both formats.

The existing six uploads changed music, covers, wording, framing, timing and publication time together. Their results identify contenders; they do not isolate why views differed. Supporting audit: output/shorts-format-audit-2026-10-12/audit-data.json.

## Story and pacing

Use a flexible sequence in either format:

1. Show an understandable incident, expectation or emotional problem immediately.
2. Establish the specific AI request or behaviour through action, brief text or dialogue appropriate to the format.
3. Show the contradiction between the expected and actual result.
4. Escalate through a new consequence, then deliver the payoff and necessary reaction.

The viewer should recognise the emotion before the clip ends. Cut repeated explanation, typing, dead time and unrelated spectacle. Strong expressions and meaningful stillness can carry the joke without constant motion.

Check both formats muted: action and expressions should make the consequence readable; captions preserve essential dialogue meaning. Useful dialogue and a verbal comic button are allowed in the dialogue format. The opening must be understandable and engaging on a phone.

## Visual benchmark: Ember

Set by the creator on 23 October 2026: **Ember (https://www.instagram.com/emberer00/) is the benchmark for visual styling, character motion, prop styling, composition and sound design.** Match how Ember builds a scene; keep our own cast, incidents and voices. Details, evidence and production numbers live in the `stable-character-animation` skill (`references/benchmark-evidence.md`, `visual-system.md`, `motion-and-audio.md`); the measured study is in `output/ember-benchmark-study-2026-10-23/`.

- **Scene:** pale cream field; sparse, faint, low-contrast scenery (wall, window, skyline, floor grid lines) that locates the scene without competing with faces.
- **Depth:** overlap, value and perspective; distant figures and objects are smaller and paler; soft contact shadows ground characters and props.
- **Characters:** confident dark outlines and flat fills; identifying clothing colour stays strong against the quiet background.
- **Selective colour:** colour goes to the characters and the object the joke depends on; inactive scenery stays muted. An important prop can gain saturation at its reveal.
- **Palette planning:** use 60–30–10 as a flexible guide, not an exact area quota or a measured Ember ratio. Preserve the dominant pale neutral field, faint environmental line art for depth, approved character colors and a small concentrated accent on the story focus. Sparse shots may have much more than 60% neutral background; never add clutter to meet the ratio. See the animation skill’s visual system.
- **Props:** every prop is held, resting or falling for a reason, with visible support and a contact shadow. Never decorative floating objects.
- **Scale:** wide shots keep characters small (about 12-20% of frame height) in sparse scenery; medium shots for acting and dialogue; close inserts for the payoff.
- **Motion (measured, 12 reels):** mostly moving on 1s (median 61% of frames change), holds for reactions (median 22% of runtime), anticipation before every impact, hard cuts between wide and inserts, drawn effects (speed lines, impact frames, shake, flashes).
- **The style break:** the top reels switch style at the payoff (painted close-up, impact frames, game/movie-parody moment), then cut back.
- **Sound (measured):** -14 LUFS master; about 2.4 sound hits per second, each on a visible change; sound in blocks with hard silences; music only as a short parody sting at the escalation; a beat of silence after the main impact. Sources and licences: the skill's `references/sound-library.md`.
- **Copy the approach, not the assets:** never reuse Ember's characters, artwork, watermark, music or audio. Original equivalents only.

## Production workflow

1. Select a real AI incident and define its behaviour, recognisable emotion and reason someone would send the clip.
2. Choose one of the two formats. Write action beats and, for dialogue, concise English lines and captions.
3. Break the episode into short shots (2–6 s each) and storyboard them in the Ember style. Use the approved cast reference.
4. Produce each shot with the `stable-character-animation` skill: generate complete whole-body drawings with the image generator (key poses, then breakdowns for big moves), assemble them locally at fixed scale over one background, and add RIFE in-betweens only on small moves after review. Grok Imagine is no longer used.
5. Edit for clear cause and effect, fast reveals and readable reactions. Use hard cuts when a changed result should appear immediately.
6. Record voices (see below), sync mouths, add music and purposeful sound effects. Caption essential dialogue, keep speech clear and duck competing sound around important moments.
7. Verify character identity, phone readability, mute comprehension, pacing and absence of engagement requests before export.

## Lessons kept from the September 2026 analysis

From the archived analyses of the first 37 uploads (August–September 2026):

- **The punchline must be visible, not only spoken.** Most viewers watch muted; the reversal should land in a face, body or image. Captions help but don't replace a visible payoff.
- **Length was not the lever.** Duration explained about 2% of view variation; 10–14 s was a mild, unproven advantage. Hence the 12–15 s working target, never at the joke's expense.
- **Average percentage viewed is misleading for Shorts**: short clips exceed 100% by looping. Judge by shares, stayed-to-watch and engaged views.
- **Comedy needs a stable normal.** AI behaviour crashing into an ordinary, recognisable situation reads as a joke; AI behaviour inside an all-AI world reads as observation. Keep settings and stakes everyday and relatable to casual users, not AI insiders.
