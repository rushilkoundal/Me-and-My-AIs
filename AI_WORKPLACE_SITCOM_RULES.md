## North star

This channel is **an animated workplace sitcom where RK's AI agents behave like insecure human coworkers, and RK — visible on screen as the protagonist — is the human they work for**.

It is not “AI news explained through cartoons.” A video must remain funny and understandable even if the viewer knows nothing about the AI announcement behind it.

Channel promise:

> What AI assistants say about us—and each other—when the chat closes.

## Brutal verdict to preserve

- The channel concept is viable; the present execution and opening hooks are the constraint.
- Viewers who enter a story often finish or rewatch it, but too many swipe before entering.
- The primary growth problem is the first-second viewing decision—not subtitles, music, hashtags, or SEO.
- Convert technical AI developments into universal human stakes: jealousy, replacement, embarrassment, incompetence, office politics, pay cuts, promotions, privacy, and unreasonable bosses.
- Build a recognizable entertainment brand rather than a collection of topical AI memes.

## Character bible

### ChatGPT / “Chad”

- Approval-seeking overachiever.
- Overexplains, projects fake confidence, hides mistakes, and panics about replacement.
- **Voice: bright, eager, helpful — never deadpan.** When Chad says something cruel it is
  always by accident, because he believes he is being useful and wants credit for it.
  Accidental cruelty is funnier than deliberate cruelty, and it keeps him likeable.
- Opening captions and labels should say **ChatGPT** for cold viewers. Other characters may call him Chad after his identity is clear.

### Claude

- Brilliant, cautious rule-follower who is quietly smug.
- Pauses, judges, carries a clipboard or equivalent original prop, and treats absurd situations seriously.

### R-One

- **Temporary character.** Not part of the default cast, not required in any episode,
  and may be dropped entirely. RK is the protagonist; R-One never competes for that.
- When he is used, his role is fixed: **RK's AI companion**, who oversees RK's other
  agents and checks that everything is actually working for RK.
- Calmly delivers catastrophic findings; the restrained, expressionless delivery makes
  the other characters’ reactions funnier.
- Do not use R-One as an inconsistent narrator, random messenger, HR representative, or
  interchangeable coworker across episodes.

### Siri

- Bitter veteran with no patience left.
- Delivers short insults and appears unimpressed.
- Supporting character, not required in every episode.

### Gemini

- Competitive employee desperate to prove Google notices him.
- Interrupts, produces charts, and exaggerates accomplishments.
- Supporting character, not required in every episode.

### RK

- **The human protagonist, fully visible on screen and in most episodes.** Not an
  off-screen voice, cursor or hand.
- The AI characters are his agents: they do his work, and they answer to him.
- Creates the crisis through unreasonable, embarrassing, lazy, or contradictory
  requests — and is just as often the one embarrassed by the result.
- Fixed design: curly black hair, stubble, white tee with a red heart, grey trousers,
  white sneakers.

Default cast: RK plus one AI agent — usually Chad or Claude. Prefer no more than two speaking characters in one Short unless the third is essential to the reversal.

## Content mix

- **60% evergreen user frustrations:** overexplaining, hallucinations, privacy, subscriptions, coding failures, laziness, emotional dependence.
- **25% AI workplace rivalry:** promotions, replacement, performance reviews, jealousy, delegation, salary, recognition.
- **15% topical AI news:** new models, features, pricing, product announcements.

Topical news may create the situation, but it cannot be the whole joke.

## Translating AI news into sitcom stakes

- Benchmark improvement → promotion rivalry.
- Larger context window → employee forced to remember everything.
- Price reduction → salary cut.
- Multiple sessions or agents → coworkers secretly delegating work.
- Privacy controversy → employee caught reading the boss’s search history.
- Usage/session limit → employee collapsing halfway through a task.
- Model launch → a new hire threatening the existing staff.
- Safety refusal → an employee terrified to approve an ordinary request.

## Required Short structure

Target length: **8–11 seconds by default**. Extend toward 15 seconds only when every extra beat earns its place.

- **0.0–0.7 seconds:** visible crisis, accusation, evidence, or consequence.
- **0.7–2.0 seconds:** identify which AI is in trouble.
- **2–5 seconds:** denial, attempted escape, or escalation.
- **5–8 seconds:** reversal or punchline.
- **8–10 seconds:** strongest reaction and immediate cut/loop.

End on the reaction. Do not leave an explanatory pause after the punchline.

## First-frame hook rules

The first second should contain at least three of these:

- Recognizable character.
- Strong visible emotion.
- Consequence.
- Accusation.
- Suspicious object or evidence.
- Specific number.
- Unanswered question.

The first frame reveals the **problem**, not the answer or full punchline.

Do not open with:

- Greetings.
- “Did you hear?”
- “Hey, Chad…”
- “But how can you…?”
- A character entering or walking into frame before anything happens.
- Technical terminology.
- A narrator explaining the news.
- Contextless dialogue.
- The entire punchline written as a caption.

## Visual storytelling rules

- Show the joke before characters explain it.
- Avoid two characters merely standing on a blank white background and trading lines.
- Give the opening a readable visual object or state: termination notice, salary slip, hidden search history, unfinished work pile, packed desk, empty “REPLACED” chair, warning notice, or performance report.
- Use expressions and body language to create contrast: RK exasperated, ChatGPT panicked, Claude quietly smug.
- Every character needs an original, distinctive silhouette and repeatable visual behavior—not only a different shirt.
- Keep the electronic-world premise simple; use props only when they communicate the conflict instantly.

## Dialogue rules

- Use natural, short spoken lines.
- One line should perform one job.
- Do not make viewers decode AI jargon, identify characters, and follow a long setup simultaneously.
- Prefer universal workplace or relationship language over product terminology.
- The punchline should change the meaning of the setup, not merely repeat it.
- If a joke requires explanation after the script is read once, simplify or reject it.

## Concept approval test

Before producing any idea, answer yes to these:

1. Is the situation understandable without knowing the related AI news?
2. Can the conflict be seen in the first frame?
3. Does the story express a universal human emotion?
4. Does each character behave according to the character bible?
5. Is the affected AI named immediately for cold viewers, and is RK's stake in it clear?
6. Is there a reversal rather than only a topical reference?
7. Can it end cleanly on a strong reaction within roughly 8–11 seconds?
8. Is it sufficiently different from recent episodes?

If fewer than six answers are yes, do not proceed without rewriting the concept.

## Originality, brand, and monetization rules

- Mention ChatGPT, Claude, Siri, and Gemini in parody dialogue when useful.
- Do not imply affiliation or endorsement.
- Channel disclaimer:

> Independent animated parody. Not affiliated with or endorsed by OpenAI, Anthropic, Google or Apple.

- Recurring characters are good; mass-produced episodes with the same staging and superficial script changes are not.
- Vary conflicts, visual evidence, outcomes, staging, and character combinations.
- Shorts are the discovery engine. Longer 2–4 minute AI Office episodes and compilations should eventually build watch hours and deeper fandom.
- Treat AI-generated music disclosure according to YouTube’s current requirements.

## Performance standards for the next test batch

Baseline diagnosed in the brutal verdict:

- Channel median stayed-to-watch problem: approximately 33.1%.
- Some Shorts had 100%+ average percentage viewed, proving the main bottleneck was entry/hook strength.

Targets:

- Median stayed to watch: **42% or higher**.
- At least three Shorts: **45% or higher stayed to watch**.
- Average percentage viewed: **95% or higher**.
- Like rate: **1.2% or higher**.
- Subscriber conversion: **1.5 or more per 1,000 views**.

Diagnosis after publishing:

- High retention + stayed-to-watch below 35% → opening/first frame still fails.
- Stayed-to-watch above 45% + weak likes/comments/subscribers → joke or characters are not memorable enough.
- Do not blame a 24-hour plateau on metadata before checking first-frame entry and engagement.

## Required creative deliverable

Whenever developing a new Short, provide:

1. Honest concept verdict and channel-fit score.
2. One-sentence human conflict.
3. First-frame visual hook and opening caption.
4. Time-coded 8–11 second script.
5. Character expressions, poses, and camera direction for every beat.
6. Punchline/reversal and loop ending.
7. Any claim that must be verified or framed explicitly as satire.

The guiding rule is:

> Build a character-driven AI workplace sitcom in which every episode is funny even to someone who missed the announcement behind it.
